Last December, I organized a week-long hackathon in Guatemala with ten friends and acquaintances of various sorts. It turns out Volaris flies from Oakland to Guatemala for the small cost of a few hundred dollars and an overnight layover. We flew into Guatemala City, drove two hours north to the historical tourist town of Antigua, and spent a week in an Airbnb working on fun side projects. I spent three days learning photography, two days climbing a volcano, and two more days writing a screenplay based on The Lottery in Babylon by Jorge Luis Borges.

I was sipping my dirty chai in an open-air café when I stumbled across Ishtar, the Sumerian goddess of love, fertility, and warfare. Ishtar is a strange figure. She was worshipped across four millennia, was the most prominent goddess in the pantheon of several ancient societies, and appears in myths in her various forms more than any other Sumerian deity. But unlike other divine queens, like Isis or Hera, Ishtar was not a mother goddess — she was something much worse. She destroyed mountains, murdered fellow goddesses, and sent divine animals to hunt her enemies. By some accounts, she was a furious, capricious goddess; by others, she simply enforced justice on those who had wronged her. She embodied sensuality, power, and passion.

Ishtar reminds me of my friends. Some love their adrenaline, pushing to hike up Acatenango, the 13,000-foot actively erupting volcano that flanks Antigua. One rode a motorcycle four hours through the mountains in the rain, slipping backwards in an attempt to climb a 45-degree slope. Several weren’t able to come on this trip; they remained in San Francisco, dutifully building their startups.

I was introduced to much of this group through the 2022 Atlas Fellowship, a program in Berkeley for high schoolers trying to change the world. It tried to equip us with the tools for understanding the world, one of those being AI. You might also not be surprised that a good number of the program’s fellows now work at startups in San Francisco.

At the house parties my friends host in SoMa, there’s a consensus that AI will soon transform the world. It’s rare that people talk about it. Some hosts even ban the topic entirely. We talk too much about AI, they say, and not enough about the world around us, the concerts we’ve been to or the best burrito spots in the city. Fair enough. I’d happily spend my life talking about iced lattes and film rankings and elderflower liqueur.

But the itch to talk about AI stems from a much deeper root: a passion for changing the world. There is a chance — a small chance, but still a chance — that my friends will develop the world’s best AI models. Some might create tools for millions of people to use or work on policy agreements affecting billions of people. Underlying that work is an understanding that they are capable enough to make those achievements happen, and a drive to spend every resource they have turning that goal into reality.

I deeply admire that passion. It’s easy to look at the empty streets of SoMa and laugh about the folks running AI agents that do their work for them. It’s easy to laugh at the enormous billboards on the highway that no one outside the tech industry really understands. Maybe some of those advertisements are misguided. But many, I think, come from people who really do want to improve the world. In a world of people ambitious for power or money’s sake, I’m happy to spend time with people ambitious in the way they help other people.

And I’m happy to spend time around people building the future. People used to dream about taking photos of every moment, playing music on demand, or even having light without fire. I can only imagine Edison was the same way — a man obsessed with his dream of a radical and wonderful technology; a human embodiment of passion.

Passion is dangerous, of course. Ishtar teaches us that love and war are deeply intertwined because they come from the same deep-seated emotions. Thankfully, this city is easy to love. I had the privilege this summer of working on a political campaign that took me to farmers’ markets, music festivals, and art fairs; to Chinese restaurants in the Richmond and cafés in North Beach; to an office where volunteers write postcards and make calls whenever they get a chance. I do my work because I want to bring more lights to this shining city on a hill.

I can only hope that my friends working seven days a week in SoMa are doing the same. But I think they are.
